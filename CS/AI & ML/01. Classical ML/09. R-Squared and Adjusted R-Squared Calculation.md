# 🧮 Hand-Calculation Walkthrough: $R^2$ & Adjusted $R^2$

> [!abstract] Executive Summary
> This note walks through a **complete step-by-step numerical calculation** of Ordinary $R^2$ (Coefficient of Determination) and **Adjusted $R^2$** on a concrete 5-student exam score dataset. It demonstrates how Total Sum of Squares ($\text{SS}_{\text{tot}}$) and Residual Sum of Squares ($\text{SS}_{\text{res}}$) are computed by hand, and why adding predictors introduces a complexity penalty.

---

## 📦 Problem Setup & Dataset

Suppose we fit a regression model with **$p = 2$ features** on **$n = 5$ students**:

- **Actual Exam Scores ($y$):** $[50, 60, 70, 80, 90]$
- **Model Predictions ($\hat{y}$):** $[55, 65, 68, 78, 84]$

### Baseline Mean ($\bar{y}$):
$$\bar{y} = \frac{50 + 60 + 70 + 80 + 90}{5} = \frac{350}{5} = \mathbf{70}$$

## 🔢 Step 1: Calculate Total Sum of Squares ($\text{SS}_{\text{tot}}$)

$$\boxed{\text{SS}_{\text{tot}} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \bar{y})^2}$$

> [!tip] Meaning of $\text{SS}_{\text{tot}}$
> How much total variance existed in the target before using our model? (Error of simply predicting the average $\bar{y} = 70$).

| Student | Actual Score ($y$) | Baseline Mean ($\bar{y}$) | Deviation ($y_i - \bar{y}$) | Squared Deviation $(y_i - \bar{y})^2$ |
| :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| **1** | 50 | 70 | $-20$ | $(-20)^2 = \mathbf{400}$ |
| **2** | 60 | 70 | $-10$ | $(-10)^2 = \mathbf{100}$ |
| **3** | 70 | 70 | $0$ | $(0)^2 = \mathbf{0}$ |
| **4** | 80 | 70 | $+10$ | $(+10)^2 = \mathbf{100}$ |
| **5** | 90 | 70 | $+20$ | $(+20)^2 = \mathbf{400}$ |
| **Sum** | — | — | — | **$\text{SS}_{\text{tot}} = \mathbf{1000}$** |

$$\text{SS}_{\text{tot}} = 400 + 100 + 0 + 100 + 400 = \mathbf{1000}$$

---

## 🔢 Step 2: Calculate Residual Sum of Squares ($\text{SS}_{\text{res}}$)

$$\boxed{\text{SS}_{\text{res}} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}$$

> [!tip] Meaning of $\text{SS}_{\text{res}}$
> How much unexplained error remains after our regression model makes its predictions?

| Student | Actual ($y$) | Predicted ($\hat{y}$) | Residual ($e = y - \hat{y}$) | Squared Error $(y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2$ |
| :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| **1** | 50 | 55 | $-5$ | $(-5)^2 = \mathbf{25}$ |
| **2** | 60 | 65 | $-5$ | $(-5)^2 = \mathbf{25}$ |
| **3** | 70 | 68 | $+2$ | $(+2)^2 = \mathbf{4}$ |
| **4** | 80 | 78 | $+2$ | $(+2)^2 = \mathbf{4}$ |
| **5** | 90 | 84 | $+6$ | $(+6)^2 = \mathbf{36}$ |
| **Sum** | — | — | — | **$\text{SS}_{\text{res}} = \mathbf{94}$** |

$$\text{SS}_{\text{res}} = 25 + 25 + 4 + 4 + 36 = \mathbf{94}$$

---

## 🎯 Step 3: Calculate Ordinary $R^2$

$$\boxed{R^2 = 1 - \frac{\text{SS}_{\text{res}}}{\text{SS}_{\text{tot}}}}$$

$$R^2 = 1 - \frac{94}{1000} = 1 - 0.094 = \mathbf{0.906} \quad (\mathbf{90.6\%})$$

> [!important] Intuitive Meaning of $R^2 = 0.906$
> Our regression model explains **$90.6\%$ of the total variation in exam scores**. Only $9.4\%$ remains unexplained residual error.

---

## 🧠 Step 4: Calculate Adjusted $R^2$ ($R^2_{\text{adj}}$)

Given:
- $n = 5$ observations
- $p = 2$ feature predictors

$$\boxed{R^2_{\text{adj}} = 1 - (1 - R^2) \left(\frac{n - 1}{n - p - 1}\right)}$$

$$R^2_{\text{adj}} = 1 - (1 - 0.906) \left(\frac{5 - 1}{5 - 2 - 1}\right)$$

$$R^2_{\text{adj}} = 1 - (0.094) \left(\frac{4}{2}\right) = 1 - (0.094 \times 2) = 1 - 0.188 = \mathbf{0.812} \quad (\mathbf{81.2\%})$$


---

## 🤔 Why Did Adjusted $R^2$ Drop from $0.906 \to 0.812$?

- **Ordinary $R^2$** only measures pure goodness-of-fit without questioning how many predictors were used.
- **Adjusted $R^2$** enforces the **Fit + Complexity Principle**:
  > *"You explained 90.6% of the variance, but you burned 2 degrees of freedom on only 5 data points."*
- Multiplier $\frac{n-1}{n-p-1} = \frac{4}{2} = 2.0$ acts as a penalty, scaling the unexplained error from $0.094 \to 0.188$.

## 💻 Python / NumPy Verification Script

```python
import numpy as np

# 1. Define vectors
y = np.array([50, 60, 70, 80, 90], dtype=float)
y_hat = np.array([55, 65, 68, 78, 84], dtype=float)
n = len(y)
p = 2

# 2. Compute Sums of Squares
ss_tot = np.sum((y - np.mean(y)) ** 2)
ss_res = np.sum((y - y_hat) ** 2)

# 3. Compute R²
r2 = 1.0 - (ss_res / ss_tot)

# 4. Compute Adjusted R²
r2_adj = 1.0 - (1.0 - r2) * ((n - 1) / (n - p - 1))

print(f"SS_tot: {ss_tot:.2f}")       # 1000.00
print(f"SS_res: {ss_res:.2f}")       # 94.00
print(f"R²:     {r2:.3f}")           # 0.906
print(f"R²_adj: {r2_adj:.3f}")       # 0.812
```

## 🔑 Key Quiz Takeaway

> [!important] Core Examination Rule
> **Why do we use Adjusted $R^2$ instead of Ordinary $R^2$?**  
> Because **Ordinary $R^2$ can artificially increase** whenever additional predictors are added (even pure noise), whereas **Adjusted $R^2$ incorporates a degrees-of-freedom penalty** to account for model complexity and feature count.
